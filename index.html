Where Do Seniors Walk in the Sun?

A street-level imagery approach to prioritising sun and rain shelter on older residents’ walking routes in Ang Mo Kio, Singapore

Abstract

Evidence on pedestrian heat exposure in Singapore is drawn from weather stations and satellite imagery, neither of which resolves conditions along individual footpaths. This study develops a screening method that ranks footpath segments on older residents’ walking routes by their exposure to sun and rain. Ten everyday walking routes in Ang Mo Kio, totalling 5.8 km, were divided into 134 segments of up to 50 m. A pre-trained semantic segmentation model (SegFormer, ADE20K) was applied to 360° street-level panoramas to derive sky openness, a proxy for sun exposure, which was combined with walking-hour rainfall frequency and existing covered-linkway coverage to produce a priority score for each segment. The analysis identifies 835 m of high-priority footpath on four routes, together with 2.1 km of tree-shaded but uncovered footpath for which a covered linkway, rather than additional planting, is the appropriate intervention. The town-centre route from Blk 207 is the most exposed under all tested variants of the method, whereas the order of individual segments is sensitive to the choice of exposure measure. The output is therefore intended as a site-check list rather than a construction list.

Keywords: pedestrian heat exposure; older adults; street-view imagery; semantic segmentation; covered linkways; Singapore

1. Problem Statement

1.1 Significance

Ang Mo Kio is one of Singapore’s oldest towns. In June 2026, 41,670 of its 157,250 residents (26.5%) were aged 65 or older, and the share approached 30% in Chong Boon and Townsville, its oldest subzones (Department of Statistics Singapore, 2026). Singapore recorded 29 days of high heat stress in 2025, and the Ministry of Sustainability and the Environment identifies the elderly as a higher-risk group (MSE, 2026).

Heat exposure is of particular concern for older pedestrians. Older adults can experience physiological heat strain without reporting greater discomfort, so resident complaints are an unreliable indicator of the most exposed stretches of footpath. Rain is a second form of exposure, as wet, uncovered paths increase the risk of slips.

1.2 Research gap

Public investment in pedestrian shelter is substantial. The Land Transport Authority (LTA) has constructed approximately 186 km of covered linkways and plans a further 50 km over five years, and the Friendly Streets programme now prioritises areas with larger elderly populations (MOT, 2025; LTA, 2025). Sites are selected primarily on the basis of distance to transit, pedestrian volume and proximity to amenities.

Evidence at the scale of the individual pedestrian is, however, limited. The National Environment Agency (NEA) operates approximately 30 Wet Bulb Globe Temperature (WBGT) stations island-wide, each located in an open setting, while satellite imagery captures rooftops and tree crowns rather than conditions at street level. Neither source can identify which section of a given walking route leaves a pedestrian exposed to direct sun. The problem addressed in this study is therefore the absence of footpath-level evidence of sun and rain exposure along the routes that older residents walk, during the hours in which they walk them.

1.3 Intended users and beneficiaries

The method is designed as a screening tool for the agencies responsible for pedestrian shelter: LTA (covered linkways, Walk2Ride and Friendly Streets), the Housing and Development Board (HDB) and the Town Council (shelters and links within housing estates), and the National Parks Board (NParks), which is responsible for street trees where a roof is not required. Final decisions remain with agency engineers following site inspection. The intended beneficiaries are the older residents who make these journeys daily.

2. Proposed Solution

This study proposes a ranked shortlist of unsheltered footpath segments, scored according to the sun and rain exposure experienced by older residents on everyday walking routes. The approach comprises three components:

  1. Representation of walking routes. Ten common trips were mapped from residential blocks to the destinations they serve (Table 1, Figure 1) and divided into 134 segments of up to 50 m, allowing exposure to be compared at the scale of a single stretch of footpath.

  2. Measurement of exposure from the pedestrian’s perspective. For each segment, the openness of the sky above the path is derived from street-level imagery, the frequency of rainfall during walking hours is estimated from station records, and existing covered-linkway coverage is determined.

  3. Ranking and explanation. The measures are combined into a single priority score and rank, and each segment is tagged with the reasons for which a site check is warranted and the type of intervention that appears appropriate.

The ranking distinguishes between two types of need. Where a footpath is exposed to both sun and rain, a covered linkway is the appropriate intervention. Where trees already shade a footpath that has no roof, the segment ranks low on sun exposure yet remains exposed to rain; such segments are reported separately as linkway candidates so that they are not overlooked in a heat-led ranking. The results are presented on an interactive site-check map (Section 4).

3. AI Methodology

Artificial intelligence is confined to the task for which no open dataset exists: interpreting what a pedestrian sees from street-level images. All subsequent steps consist of arithmetic and GIS operations that can be audited by a planner. 3.1 Data: open street-level imagery

KartaView 360° panoramas captured in 2022 form the basis of the ranking. For each segment midpoint, the nearest panorama with heading metadata was retained and rotated to true north using that heading. A total of 131 panoramas cover all 134 segments at a median distance of 9 m from the segment midpoint. Fifteen segments rely on a panorama located more than 25 m away; these segments were retained but flagged for site verification.

Mapillary street photographs provide independent validation from a second camera. Of 1,447 photographs located near the routes, 545 lying on the ten routes were retained, levelled and classified by viewing direction (forward, side or rear). Almost all were captured in January 2017.

Both sources are openly licensed (CC BY-SA) and can be re-processed by an agency at no cost, allowing the method to be applied beyond the study area.

3.2 Semantic segmentation

Semantic segmentation was employed to identify the physical elements influencing pedestrian exposure to sunlight along the selected walking routes. Unlike conventional image classification, which assigns a single label to an image, semantic segmentation classifies individual pixels, allowing the relative proportions of sky, vegetation, and surrounding built structures to be quantified. This provides a consistent method of translating street-level imagery into measurable indicators of pedestrian exposure.

The study adopted SegFormer (Xie et al., 2021), a transformer-based semantic segmentation model, using its B0 variant pre-trained on the ADE20K dataset (Zhou et al., 2017). The model distinguishes 150 semantic classes, including sky, trees, buildings, and road surfaces. These were consolidated into broader categories relevant to the study: sky, tree or vegetation, building, roof, and other solid surfaces. Particular emphasis was placed on distinguishing sky from vegetation (Figure 2), as their relative proportions form the basis of the sky openness indicator used in the subsequent exposure analysis.

The segmentation workflow involved processing the upper hemisphere of each KartaView panorama in four 512 × 512 image tiles. Each tile was classified using the pre-trained model to generate pixel-level segmentation masks. Black pixels in the original imagery were treated as unknown rather than shaded areas to avoid interpreting missing image information as physical obstruction. The resulting classifications were subsequently used to calculate view factors, accounting for the relative contribution of different parts of the visible sky dome.

SegFormer was selected for its computational efficiency, accessibility, and ability to distinguish multiple elements of urban streetscapes without additional model training. This was particularly relevant given the absence of a locally annotated dataset for Singapore’s HDB environments. Although the model could identify vegetation and buildings, its recognition of covered walkways and smaller overhead structures was less reliable. Consequently, segmentation was used primarily to estimate sky and canopy visibility, while existing covered-linkway information was obtained separately from LTA’s spatial dataset.

Overall, semantic segmentation provided a reproducible method for extracting pedestrian-level environmental characteristics from publicly available imagery. Rather than directly predicting thermal comfort, the model generates spatial indicators that support the subsequent calculation of sky openness and identification of potentially exposed footpath segments. The consistency of these measurements was tested against a second image source (Section 3.7).

3.3 Sky openness as a proxy for sun exposure

For each segmented panorama, the proportions of sky, tree and building in the upper half of the view were computed, weighted so that pixels near the zenith contribute in proportion to their share of the sky dome. Sky openness is defined as:

openness = sky ÷ (sky + tree)

A value of 1 indicates a fully open sky and 0 indicates complete tree-canopy cover; the classified upward view of the highest-ranked segment is shown in Figure 12 (Appendix E). Buildings are excluded from the ratio, so that the measure isolates the shade provided by trees relative to open sky.

A separate adjustment was applied for covered linkways. Where the LTA covered-linkway layer (LTA DataMall, September 2026) intersects a segment (within a 3 m tolerance):

effective openness = openness × U

where U is the proportion of the segment without mapped roof coverage. A fully covered segment therefore receives an effective openness score of 0.

An hour-by-hour sun model was also developed (Section 3.7), which projects the solar position at each walking hour of 2026 onto each north-aligned panorama and classifies the hour as sunlit or shaded. A substantial proportion of hours could not be classified, because panoramas frequently omit the sky directly overhead; at certain hours, fewer than one third of route length had a valid observation. Sky openness was therefore adopted because it can be computed for every segment, is readily explained and can be validated against a second camera, although it describes the sky above a path rather than the position of the sun at a given hour.

3.4 Rain exposure

Covered walkways serve as essential infrastructure for ensuring that the elderly can travel safely during rainy weather. This study evaluates the level of rain exposure along existing pedestrian routes by overlaying rainfall data with the lengths of uncovered sections. The analysis utilises rainfall data from the National Environment Agency (NEA) and covered walkway data from the Land Transport Authority (LTA), processed via overlay analysis in ArcGIS.

Taking October 2026 as the sample period, a nearest-neighbour analysis was employed to identify the three weather stations closest to each of the 134 segments, whose rainfall frequencies were blended by inverse-distance weighting. The study then calculated the proportion of 5-minute intervals with recorded rainfall during two specific time windows: 07:00–10:00 and 16:00–19:00. The Rain Exposure Score for each of the 134 segments was calculated by multiplying the rainfall frequency for each segment by its uncovered length (Figure 3); route-level maps are provided in Figure 13 (Appendix E).

Existing LTA covered linkways occur only in short stretches, at both ends of R06, near the end of R03, at the start of R08, and on parts of R07 (S09–S10, near the bus interchange) and R10 (S02), and do not form a continuous covered system. R06 and R10 record the highest rainfall frequency, but once cover and length are accounted for, R04 (12.7) and R05 (12.4) carry the greatest rain exposure, as they are long and entirely uncovered; R07 records the lowest (6.7) owing to the covered stretch near the bus interchange. R04 and R05 are therefore the priority candidates for improved sheltered connectivity.

For the ranking, rain exposure is expressed per metre (rainfall frequency × uncovered share) rather than as a total, so that segments of different lengths can be compared on an equal basis (Section 3.5).

3.5 Ranking

Both measures were normalised to the range 0–1 across the 134 segments (minimum–maximum scaling), so that neither dominates by virtue of its units, and combined as follows:

priority = 0.7 × openness + 0.3 × rain

Segments were ranked from 1 to 134 and classified as Low (below 0.4; 21 segments), Medium (0.4–0.7; 94 segments) or High (0.7 and above; 19 segments). Route scores are length-weighted means. Each segment additionally carries data-quality and intervention flags: panorama located more than 25 m away; no LTA linkway present, requiring verification of estate shelters; and linkway candidate (openness below 0.35 with no cover).

A weighting of 70/30 was adopted for two reasons. First, heat poses the greater health risk to older adults. Second, sun exposure varies considerably more between segments than rainfall does: the coefficient of variation is 48% for openness and 8% for the rainfall rate. Rainfall nonetheless distinguishes covered from uncovered segments. As the weighting involves judgement, its sensitivity was tested: every weighting from 50/50 to 100/0 retains 19 or 20 of the same top 20 segments, and R05-S02 ranks first in every case (Appendix D).

3.6 Heat context: reference WBGT and direct-sun exposure

The following heat measures do not enter the priority score. They establish background heat-stress conditions and provide an independent measure of direct sun against which the sky-openness ranking is checked (Section 3.7).

Wet Bulb Globe Temperature (WBGT) data were obtained from the National Environment Agency (NEA) via the data.gov.sg API to assess background heat-stress conditions during assumed walking periods of 07:00–10:00 and 16:00–19:00. 15-minute readings from nearby monitoring stations at Yio Chu Kang Stadium, Bishan Street and MacRitchie Reservoir were aggregated into hourly means. Observations from 5 November 2026 were used as a supplementary assessment rather than a representation of annual heat conditions. The eight hourly reference WBGT measurements ranged from 24.13 °C to 28.02 °C, with the highest hourly mean observed at 09:00. The morning average was 26.94 °C, compared with 25.08 °C in the evening, and no hour reached NEA’s “moderate” threshold of 31 °C (Figure 6, Appendix E). These measurements represent heat-stress conditions at the monitoring stations, rather than the thermal conditions experienced along individual footpaths.

A separate assessment of direct sunlight was conducted using date-specific solar classification for all 134 footpath segments. Each observation was classified as sunlit, shaded or unavailable, with unknown and night-time classifications excluded. Direct-sun exposure was calculated as:

direct-sun exposure (%) = (sunlit observations ÷ valid observations) × 100

Segment-level exposure percentages were aggregated into length-weighted route means. Of the 1,072 segment-hour records, 798 had usable solar classifications. R02 (33.53%), R05 (33.33%) and R04 (32.17%) had the highest modelled direct-sun exposure, while R01 had the lowest (20.67%) (Figure 7, Appendix E).

The direct-sun assessment supplemented the primary ranking’s sky-openness indicator. NEA WBGT observations established reference heat-stress conditions during selected walking hours, while modelled direct-sun percentages provided an additional measure of spatial exposure.

3.7 Validation

As the ranking rests on a single image source and a single proxy, it was compared with three independent measures that do not enter the score:

  • Second image source. To assess the consistency of the segmentation-derived measurements, the approach was also applied to an independent set of Mapillary street photographs. Of the 545 photographs retained along the study routes, 384 met the validation criteria (within 20 m of the route and with less than 10% vehicle pixels), covering 66 segments. Comparison with the KartaView-derived openness measurements produced a Pearson correlation of 0.59 and a Spearman correlation of 0.70, with stronger agreement along R01–R03 (r = 0.81–0.86). These results indicate reasonable consistency across image sources, although differences in camera perspective, image capture year, and vegetation conditions introduce uncertainty.

  • Annual shade by hour. For every date in 2026 and each walking hour, the solar ray was projected onto the north-aligned classified panorama and classified as sunlit, tree-shaded or building-shaded. The correlation with openness is r = 0.44, and R05 records the lowest annual walking-hour shade of any route (64.6%; Figure 11, Appendix E).

  • Single-day direct sun. The direct-sun model for 5 November 2026 (Section 3.6) correlates with openness at r = 0.27; R02 (33.5%) and R05 (33.3%) are its most exposed routes.

The NEA WBGT readings reported in Section 3.6 remained below the “moderate” threshold throughout the walking hours. As differences between footpaths depend on whether direct sun reaches them rather than on background temperature, the method ranks exposure rather than temperature.

4. Prototype and Results

4.1 Project page and site-check map

The results are published on a project landing page that includes an interactive map of all 134 segments, coloured by priority score (Figure 4). It is designed to support site inspections and provides:

  • Filters by route, priority band, linkway candidacy and a site-check list (high priority, linkway candidate, or panorama more than 25 m away), with labels for origins, destinations, routes, roads and segments.

  • A segment panel reporting scores, coverage, coordinates and reasons for inspection, with links to Google Maps and Street View and navigation along the route.

  • Field-note entry, a downloadable CSV field sheet, a printable checklist and geolocation for use on site.

4.2 Ranking results

R05, from Blk 207 to the Town Centre, is the most exposed route. Its length-weighted openness is 0.84 and its route score 0.85, compared with 0.58 for the next-ranked routes (R02 and R10). Fourteen of the twenty highest-ranked segments lie on R05, three on R10, two on R02 and one on R03. The least exposed route is R06 (openness 0.22), which is short and lined with trees. Route-level values for all measures are reported in Appendix C.

4.3 Priorities for site inspection

Nineteen segments score 0.7 or above, comprising 835 m of footpath on R05, R10, R02 and R03 (Figure 5, Table 3). In addition, 48 segments totalling 2.1 km are shaded by trees but uncovered. Because trees already provide shade, these segments rank low overall, yet they offer no protection from rain; a covered linkway rather than further planting is the appropriate intervention.

4.4 Route-level findings

R05 (Blk 207 → Town Centre; 677 m; no linkway). S02 is the most exposed segment in the study area (score 0.96; openness 0.99), and S07 is the only shaded stretch. Five segments rely on panoramas 25–29 m away, and the 2017 photographs show more tree cover than the 2022 panoramas; R05 is therefore the first priority for inspection.

R02 (Blk 180 → 628 Market; 637 m; no linkway). An open stretch of 350 m (S05–S11) scores 0.64–0.71 (ranks 18–29), whereas S04 lies under tree cover (rank 122). Both image sources agree, making this the best-supported case for shade.

R04 (Blk 244 → Blk 226A–G shops; 726 m, the longest route; no linkway). Trees shade S02–S04, S06 and S07, giving a route openness of 0.39, and no segment ranks within the top 20. R04 nevertheless records the highest rain total of any route. Sun and rain exposure therefore diverge: five segments (250 m) are linkway candidates, for which a roof rather than additional trees is indicated.

R01 (Blk 612 → Community Hospital; 595 m; no linkway). The route is open at both ends (S02 scores 0.64) and tree-lined in its middle section (S09 scores 0.32). The open ends warrant shade; the middle section warrants a linkway. Route maps for R01, R02, R04 and R05 are provided in Figures 9 and 10 (Appendix E).

5. Critical Evaluation

5.1 Robustness of results

The route-level result is robust. R05 is the most exposed route under either image source, under every weighting from 50/50 to 100/0, and when either direct-sun model is substituted for openness; it also records the lowest annual shade in the full-year model. The weighting has little effect on the segment shortlist, with 19–20 of the top 20 segments retained.

The segment-level order is less certain. The two image sources agree on most routes but diverge on R05, where the 2022 panoramas indicate openness of 0.75–0.95 and the 2017 photographs approximately 0.4 (Figure 8, Appendix E). When photograph-based openness is substituted where available, R05 retains 8 rather than 14 of the top 20 segments while remaining the highest-ranked route. Substituting a direct-sun model for openness retains only 6–7 of the top 20 segments. The moderate correlations (r = 0.27–0.44) reflect related but distinct quantities: openness describes the whole sky above a path, whereas the direct-sun models test a single solar ray at specific hours. The method therefore reliably identifies which routes require attention, but individual segments must be confirmed on site, which is why the output is framed as a site-check list rather than a list of sites for construction.

5.2 Limitations

  • Absence of site verification. No site visits were conducted. All results derive from imagery and open data and have not been verified on the ground.

  • Incomplete shelter data. Only the LTA covered-linkway layer was available. HDB and Town Council walkways are not mapped, so segments recorded as uncovered are unverified rather than confirmed as open.

  • Street-level rather than footpath-level imagery. Images are captured from the carriageway, at a median distance of 9 m from the segment midpoint; 15 panoramas lie more than 25 m away, and panorama headings were not verified in the field.

  • Temporal mismatch of imagery. Photographs date mainly from 2017 and panoramas from 2022; the two disagree on R05, and current tree cover has not been verified.

  • Model accuracy. SegFormer was not trained on HDB estates, its accuracy was not evaluated against manually labelled local images, and it rarely identifies linkway roofs, which is why cover is taken from the LTA layer.

  • Assumed walking hours and a short rainfall record. Walking windows of 07:00–10:00 and 16:00–19:00 are assumed rather than surveyed, and the rainfall record covers approximately one month.

  • Exposure rather than temperature. No field WBGT measurements were taken; openness is a proxy for sun exposure, and the 70/30 weighting is a tested judgement.

  • Route selection. The ten routes represent common trips by older residents but were not sampled from travel data; they demonstrate the method rather than provide town-wide coverage.

5.3 Challenges

The principal challenge was the selection of a defensible heat-exposure measure. The initial approach, hour-by-hour classification of sun and shade from each panorama, could not classify a substantial proportion of hours because panoramas omit the sky overhead; sky openness was therefore adopted and the hourly models retained for validation. Integration also required a common segment geometry across six contributors and the reconciliation of imagery from two capture years.

5.4 Potential improvements

  1. Inspect the 15 flagged segments and the whole of R05, recording cover, tree canopy and surface conditions using the prototype’s field sheet.

  2. Map HDB and Town Council walkways in collaboration with the Town Council, so that uncovered segments can be confirmed.

  3. Measure WBGT in the field on high- and low-ranked segments to test whether openness corresponds to experienced heat stress.

  4. Evaluate model accuracy against manually labelled local images, and acquire full-zenith imagery so that every hour can be classified.

  5. Extend the rainfall record to several years, and base walking hours and route selection on survey or travel data for older residents.

  6. Scale the method: as it relies only on open data, a lead agency could apply it town-wide annually or whenever new imagery becomes available.
